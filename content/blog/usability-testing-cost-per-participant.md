Usability testing cost per participant is rarely the number on a panel's price page: the incentive is one line on a five-line bill. Picture the receipt for one completed 60-minute moderated session with a general consumer. The panel prints one or two of its lines, while the moderator's hours, the analysis and the empty slot a no-show leaves behind stay off it. This guide prices each line from named sources, then moves the unit from one head to one test round, which is how our [usability testing services](/services/usability-testing-services/) are scoped.

## What is the full usability testing cost per participant?

**The full usability testing cost per participant is the sum of five lines: incentive, recruiting, a no-show buffer, researcher time and tools.**

A panel or recruiter quote prices the first two lines at most. Moderation, analysis and tooling arrive as hours on your own calendar, so they slip out of budgets; the receipt below prints researcher time as two lines, moderation and analysis.

## How much should you pay usability test participants?

**Set the incentive by audience and session length: start from what moderated consumer studies pay per hour, then scale up for specialists and down for short unmoderated tasks.**

[User Interviews' guide to research incentives](https://www.userinterviews.com/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-user-research-incentives) reports that about three in four completed moderated consumer sessions on its platform, all in the US and Canada, paid $60–$100 per hour, and $100 was the most common rate, at 25% of sessions.

A usability test participant incentive for a short unmoderated task is smaller. [GitLab's published research incentive policy](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/product/ux/ux-research-coordination/participation-gratuities/) pays its own participants $20 for an unmoderated test of up to 10 minutes and $40 for 25–30 minutes, against $120 for a 60-minute interview.

Underpaying rarely saves money: in User Interviews' study data, higher incentives slightly reduced no-show rates, as the no-show section below shows.

## What does recruiting add to each participant?

**Recruiting adds either a per-head fee or staff hours, and the amount climbs with how rare and how busy your participants are.**

Usability testing recruitment cost depends on the channel:

- **Research panel.** [MeasuringU's breakdown of usability test costs](https://measuringu.com/usability-cost/) lists panels from about $7–$10 per participant for a general consumer profile and $40–$100 for specialized profiles such as financial advisors. Ask whether that price includes the incentive, so you do not budget it twice.
- **Recruiting agency.** [Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g)'s article on recruiting test participants](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recruiting-test-participants-for-usability-studies/) reported an average agency recruiting fee of $107 per participant in its original survey, run in 2002, so treat it as a floor and get current quotes. Ask whether the fee is per recruited head or per completed session, because no-shows change the answer.
- **Your own customer list.** There is no fee, but your staff spend hours finding, screening and scheduling people; the FAQs give NN/g's figure.

Every channel needs a screener: the short questionnaire that filters applicants on behavior, devices and role. In our engagements, participant recruitment is a 5–8 day step before the first round of sessions.

1. **General consumer:** online research panel; hard part is filtering out professional testers; the baseline, a 5–8 day step in our process.

NN/g's guidance on usability testing with older adults recommends a screener that asks about technology experience and the devices people own, and stresses building trust in the session. When the team behind a 2021 study in JMIR mHealth and uHealth ("Going Remote") moved such testing to remote sessions, it mailed care packages to participants, and some needed a smartphone or home internet supplied: cost lines no panel quote shows. Clinicians are recruited around shift schedules, which spreads sessions across more calendar days.

Place each user group on the ladder before you request quotes, and ask every recruiter for the lead time at that step.

## Why do no-shows raise the cost of each session?

**Because you pay to recruit people who never join and to hold their slots, and those costs spread across the sessions that do happen.**

[MeasuringU's analysis of no-show rates](https://measuringu.com/typical-no-show-rate-for-moderated-studies/) blends User Interviews data from about 14,000 studies (9% no-show) with its own operations data (5%) into an average of about 8%, and treats 10% as a conservative planning figure in place of its older 10–20% guidance. The same data shows higher incentives slightly reduce no-shows, and MeasuringU's over-recruiting advice treats no-shows as only one of the ways a booked session is lost.

So the real usability testing cost per participant includes the people who never show up:

> **Cost per completed session (illustrative formula)** = (recruited heads × recruiting fee + incentives paid + moderator hours held × your rate) ÷ completed sessions

Over-recruiting is the fix: book one or two floaters per user group, so a missed slot does not cost you a session, and settle what each one is paid (see the FAQs).

## How many researcher hours sit behind each session?

**More hours than the session itself, because a moderated hour needs setup before it and a debrief, notes, clips and severity ratings after it.**

In our moderated usability testing, a researcher runs 45–60 minute think-aloud sessions over Lookback or Zoom, for example on a clickable Figma prototype, and every participant answers the System Usability Scale (SUS) and the Single Ease Question (SEQ). In the usability test report, every finding gets a video clip, a severity rating and a recommended design fix. Put your own hours against each segment below, then multiply by your loaded rate.

<details class="astext"><summary>Show as text</summary>

- **Setup:** pilot the tasks; check the prototype link, recording and consent form.
- **Session:** the 45–60 minute think-aloud session.
- **Debrief:** capture the top observations while they are fresh.
- **Notes and clips:** timestamped notes and a video clip per finding.
- **Severity and fix proposal:** a 1–4 rating and a recommended design fix per finding.

</details>

Unmoderated usability testing removes the moderator's hour, not the analysis: each unmoderated study in Maze or UserTesting needs more participants, every recording still needs watching, and nobody can ask a follow-up question when someone stalls.

If nobody on your side can give a round these hours, that is the part of the bill our usability testing engagement takes off your calendar.

## Why is cost per participant the wrong unit for quotes?

**Because a per-head price says nothing about what happens after the sessions, so compare quotes per round: recruit, test, analyze, fix the design and retest.**

The cost per usability test round is the number to compare: two quotes for "5 users" can describe a set of unmoderated clips or a moderated round with analysis and design fixes. Once you know your usability testing cost per participant, multiply it by a round, not by a study; in our process, round 2 uses new participants and measures the fixed design against the round 1 task success rate.

Four questions put any two quotes on the same footing:

1. **How many participants per round and per user group?** A good answer gives both, so two user groups are not quoted as one.
2. **Moderated, unmoderated or both?** A good answer names the tool and session length, because a 15-minute recording and a 60-minute moderated session are different products.
3. **Who analyzes and writes the report?** A good answer names a report where each finding has a clip and a severity rating.
4. **Are design fixes and a retest included?** A good answer says who changes the design file between rounds and whether round 2 recruits new people.

Ask all four in writing, then set each provider's answers in one column before you look at any total.

## What does a round cost when you run it yourselves?

**Add the incentive, recruiting and researcher time for each completed session, then the no-show buffer and tool cost, then the design hours spent fixing what the round found.**

The last two rows are the ones a per-head panel price leaves out, and they decide whether the round changed anything. If the design-fix row has no owner, settle who fixes the design before you book a session.

## FAQs

### What happens to the incentive when a participant does not show up?

It follows the policy you write before recruiting, because no published norm settles it. Decide three cases: a no-show, a late cancellation inside an agreed window, and a floater on standby. Put each answer into the invitation and consent text, so nobody argues about payment afterward.

### Is unmoderated testing cheaper per participant?

Yes, per head, because no moderator sits in the session and shorter tasks carry smaller incentives. Our unmoderated Maze and UserTesting studies run 20–50 participants, though, and every recording still needs review, so price the round rather than the head.

### Does testing with older adults or clinicians cost more?

Yes. Older adults need a screener built around their device habits and, in remote sessions, sometimes mailed care packages, as in the JMIR study above. Clinicians are booked around shift schedules, so ask each recruiter for a quote and lead time per group rather than applying a vendor's premium.

### Can we recruit our own customers to cut the cost?

Yes, and it removes the recruiting fee, but the cost moves into staff time: NN/g's respondents spent an average of 1.15 staff hours per participant recruited. Your list also leans toward loyal users, so add outsiders when a round tests first-time tasks, and still offer an incentive or a credit.

### How many participants should we budget for per round?

Our rounds use 5–8 participants per user group, so a product with two distinct groups budgets each group separately and adds one or two floaters per group. If you cannot yet name your user groups, a round of [discovery-focused UX research](/services/ux-research-services/) defines them before the first test is booked.
